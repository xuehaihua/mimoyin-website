How to Specify ePTFE Membrane for Battery Explosion-Proof Vent Pads

Scope: this article covers the membrane material used inside an explosion-proof vent pad, not a finished valve or vent-pad assembly. Every value below is a material-level test range; final parts are matched to the customer's drawing.

1. The failure that starts the specification review

A battery enclosure passes its initial water test, then a summer heat cycle leaves the pack shell visibly bulged. Another batch passes pressure relief but takes on water after a wash-down. In both cases the vent pad was bought as a catalogue item, and nobody checked whether the membrane behind the port had the right airflow-to-water-entry balance for that specific housing.

Four recurring complaints drive most redesigns:

  • Shell swelling — gas generated during charge/discharge cannot escape fast enough through an under-venting membrane.
  • Water ingress — a high-airflow membrane with low water-entry pressure is used on a pack that sees pressure washing or standing water.
  • Edge lift after thermal cycling — the adhesive system softens or creeps before the membrane itself ages out.
  • Airflow drift — dust, salt mist or oil contaminates the exposed face and slowly blocks the microporous path.

2. Compare materials with one measurement method

Airflow in this table is ml/min at a Ø10 mm orifice under 7 kPa. It is the total flow through a fixed test port, not a per-area value; a number without the orifice and pressure is not comparable.

Engineering trade-off: higher airflow generally means lower water-entry pressure. PU is cheaper and fluorine-free has an environmental story; ePTFE is chosen when temperature, chemical exposure or consistent microporous structure dominates. IP67/IP68 is a result of the whole enclosure, not a membrane rating.

3. Four purchasing traps

  1. Comparing airflow units blindly. A per-area value and a total-flow value through a Ø10 mm port differ by orders of magnitude. Ask for the test orifice and differential pressure.
  2. Treating IP rating as a membrane specification. Housing seal, gasket compression and adhesive bonding decide the final result as much as the film does.
  3. Mixing up adhesive temperature and film temperature. A membrane rated to +260 °C does not make its backing adhesive survive the same temperature.
  4. Assuming a standard disc fits every port. Port geometry, adhesive ring width and die-cut tolerance must be taken from the customer drawing.

4. How a vent-pad membrane is actually validated

We do not publish pack-level claim data. A typical validation follows the customer's interface drawing:

  • Confirm port diameter, available bonding land and adhesive system, then die-cut a sample lot to that outline.
  • Report membrane airflow at Ø10 mm / 7 kPa and water-entry pressure as material-level values, and state that they are not finished-part results.
  • Run high/low-temperature cycling, salt mist and aging on the bonded membrane, then check airflow retention and edge lift.
  • Send sample lots for the customer's own enclosure-level verification; adjust grade only against measured results.

Our custom die-cut ePTFE membrane process handles round, rectangular and irregular outlines per drawing.

5. Specification checklist before you release a drawing

  • Airflow: target range at Ø10 mm / 7 kPa, stated as custom per spec.
  • Water entry: material-level water column matched to the worst-case exposure.
  • Adhesive: chemical family, temperature limit and bond width called out separately from film temperature.
  • Die-cut: outline tolerance and liner/winding requirements from the assembly drawing.
  • Aging: salt mist, thermal cycling and damp heat conditions agreed before volume order.
  • Documentation: RoHS report and lot-level test records. We do not claim REACH certification.
